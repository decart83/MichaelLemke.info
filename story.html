My story

From proofs to pipelines

Twenty years in, my career looks like a plan. It wasn’t one. It was a series of curiosities I refused to let go of.

Two degrees, one habit of mind

I studied mathematics and philosophy at Northern Michigan University. The two are the same discipline pointed in different directions: both hand you a hard question and demand you show your work. I presented papers on epistemic logic and permutation groups, founded the Philosophy Club, and won an award for improving how the campus learned. The habit stuck — take something ambiguous, make it rigorous, make it useful.

The textbook becomes software

My first decade was spent where math meets students — Houghton Mifflin while I was still finishing my degrees, then digital R&D, then four years at Pearson managing the lifecycle of five hundred–plus digital products: interactive books, test generators, video games, assessment systems. This was the era when the textbook became software, and I had a front-row seat.

In the middle of all this I spent a year as the one-man technology department at a Chicago startup called Uncommon — servers, networks, fulfillment architecture, and campaigns with major consumer-tech, photo, and gaming brands. A startup teaches you that every problem is your problem. I never unlearned that.

The data turn

At McGraw-Hill I crossed from making products to deciding which products got made — a platform roadmap of 556 courses across 79 disciplines. I learned that the fastest way to win a roadmap argument is to bring data. A sales-activity analysis I piloted helped one team grow revenue 43% year over year, and that thread kept pulling: at Area9 Lyceum I built a team of 23 learning engineers shipping more than a hundred adaptive courses for healthcare, airline, pharmaceutical, and public-sector organizations.

Then ETS gave me the problems I’d been training for. I built fraud analytics that rank suspicious behavior by risk and confidence, turning test integrity from reactive to proactive. I coded a facial-detection system in Python that sharply cut security costs. And I established the ETS Center of Intelligence — data governance, self-service analytics, fourteen automated workflows — so the whole business could see itself clearly. Somewhere in there the throughline became obvious: I don’t just like analyzing things. I like building systems that make analysis unavoidable.

Strategy, at scale

Alteryx brought me into the data industry itself. I built industry and line-of-business solution blueprints used with Fortune 500 companies worldwide, stood up the content platform that delivered them, and later served as RevOps business partner to an organization of roughly five hundred people — planning, KPIs, and operating rhythms. I put GenAI into production early, with an OpenAI-powered pipeline that turned scattered customer anecdotes into a searchable library of use cases. The company named me Operational Leader of the Year.

Today I’m at Snowflake, leading strategy and operations for Solution Engineering as a partner to the Field CTO organization across AI/ML, Data Engineering, and Apps & Collaboration. Bigger stage, same job: take ambiguity, apply rigor, hand back clarity.

Off the clock

The building doesn’t stop at five. I’ve shipped iOS apps, built websites for a historical society, a poetry foundation, and an artist, and served four years on a school board — passing the love of learning that started this story on to my kids. Somewhere in Lansdale, PA, there is always a dataset open and a side project in progress. That feels right.
